## Ruyant 2026: the one that needs a real answer

**His question is not ours, and that is exactly why he is dangerous.** He asks what LLMs *represent*, treating them as epistemic vehicles and applying philosophy-of-science accounts of representation (content-views, intention-views, norm-views).

**His conclusion:** norm-views win, and on them "they each represent a certain class of appropriate linguistic production" -- a constructed class, "not a natural class of linguistic phenomena," defined by "norms of appropriateness established by LLM makers." Not the world. Not even the structure of natural language.

Then the sharp part. In practice the instances are imaginary: "When discussing with a chatbot, we should consider that we are engaged in a fiction... the outputs of LLMs serve as props in a game of make-believe, and only our imagination is responsible for providing hypothetical referential links to the world." He converges with Mallory's (2023) fictionalism, and notes the convergence is evidence, since he got there by a different route.

### The objection this puts to our paper

If LLM outputs are props in make-believe, then asking after their *truth-tracking profile* looks like a category error before it starts. You don't audit a novel's answerability. A referee who has read Ruyant can ask why §7 treats LLM outputs as candidates for answerability at all.

This is the most serious objection surfaced in the whole pass, and it is not answered anywhere in the current draft.

### The answer, and it is his own

**Ruyant hands it over: fictions can be performative.** His words:

> "the fictional texts generated by LLMs can become actual texts: student dissertations, professional emails, motivation letters, computer programs, news or scientific articles or paragraphs within them, authored by the human agents responsible for their deployment."

The moment a generated text is appropriated and deployed, it stops being a prop and becomes an assertion someone is answerable for. That transition is precisely where our stabilizer profile does its work, and Ruyant has no account of it: he moves straight from "fictions can be performative" to the ethics of chatbot deployment, without asking what makes a performative appropriation *safe*. Our profile answers that question. The point is not that he is wrong; it is that his analysis stops one step short of the one that matters practically, and his own ethical worries ("applications for which LLMs are fundamentally untrustworthy") presuppose an answerability notion he never supplies.

Recommend a short §6 objection-and-reply, framed as extension rather than refutation. This is Rapoport-compliant and it strengthens the paper: it gives the profile a job that a well-worked-out rival account leaves undone.

### Is Ruyant simply wrong? Three candidate objections, assessed 2026-08-08

Brett's reaction, sight unseen: the account may be out of date; LLM output is highly processed information corresponding closely with the world, especially at the frontier; and users don't treat it as fiction. Assessed against the text:

**1. Out of date: yes, and evidencably so.** Word-boundary counts over the full text: `retrieval` 0, `RAG` 0 (the 11 apparent hits are *pragmatic*, *paragraph*, *Mirages*), `verif*` 0, `agentic` 0, `grounding` 0, `reliab*` 0; `tool` twice and `web` three times, neither in the tool-use sense. His working object is a text predictor plus fine-tuning. Received 7 January 2025, accepted 15 December 2025. **But he is scoped, not refuted:** the generalisation is stated as holding "to the extent that they are not trained on direct 'worldly' inputs." That hedge is load-bearing, and it is also, in effect, a concession of our thesis in his vocabulary. Worth quoting as such.

**2. Correspondence with the world: the weakest of the three; do not run it.** Ruyant's claim concerns the representation relation, not accuracy, and he has already absorbed factual correctness into appropriateness: the represented class depends on "linguistic, conceptual, **factual**, practical, moral and aesthetic constraints." A frontier model that is right about the world nearly always is, on his view, extremely good at producing appropriate linguistic production. Rising accuracy leaves him untouched.

The upgraded version is ours, not his: the issue is not the *level* of correspondence but **what anchors the norm**. Where appropriateness is enforced by code execution, retrieval that must cite a resolvable source, or verifiable reward, the norm ceases to be maker-stipulated and becomes world-anchored, which is a stabilizer being added. Note the methodological point for our own drafting: "correspondence is increasingly high" is exactly the bare correlational claim the profile view exists to replace with route-specific predictions.

**3. Users don't treat it as fiction: strongest, and it locates an internal tension.** Ruyant needs incompatible things. Defending the norm-view he writes that people "only converse with LLMs as if they were agents in a playful or instrumental way without taking it seriously." Motivating his ethics he holds that users are deluded into treating them as agents, urgently so. On norm-views what is represented is fixed by "makers, **entrenched usage**, or institutions" -- his own disjunction -- so if entrenched usage treats outputs as world-directed assertions, the view may not deliver his answer.

His available reply, which any version of this objection has to beat: norms are normative, not statistical, and he says outright that vendors "might lead us astray by inciting misuse." Mass misuse is not norm change. So the objection must run through **institutional uptake** (courts, clinicians, journals, firms writing LLM-assisted output into their procedures), which is norm formation, not aggregate error.

### Amendment (same day): why (2) is a better objection than assessed above

Brett's follow-up: "is there anything that's right about the world 100% of the time? Anything at all?" No. Perception has illusions, every instrument carries a tolerance, testimony is fallible, theories get replaced; only self-verifying or analytic claims never fail, and they are uninformative about the world.

That converts the assessment above. It remains true that rising accuracy does not *refute* the representation claim. But if nothing reaches 100%, then "compatible with being right nearly all the time" means Ruyant's account is compatible with **every** level of accuracy anything has achieved, so accuracy does no work in it whatever. It cannot separate an LLM from a thermometer, a textbook, or a working scientist.

He may accept that, at two costs. First, the whole weight shifts to the norm claim, where objections (1) and (3) already press. Second, and unpayable: his ethics turns on LLMs being "fundamentally **untrustworthy**" for certain applications, which is a reliability notion, and the paper has no reliability vocabulary at all. Full-text counts: `reliab*` 0, `calibrat*` 0, `fallib*` 0, `mistake` 0, `check` 0, `error` 1, against `untrustworthy` 1 in the ethics section. **He imports for his normative conclusion the notion his semantics disowns.** That is the wedge, and it is stronger than anything in the correspondence framing.

### The positive point this yields, and it is the paper's thesis

What distinguishes a thermometer from a hallucinating chatbot is not infallibility, since the thermometer errs too. It is **calibration**: a route by which errors are caught and corrected, and a tolerance that can be stated. Truth-tracking is not being right; it is being correctible. Arguing the correspondence line concedes the terrain to accuracy when our claim is about correction routes.

**Unifying finding across the whole pass: none of the five rivals has a correction notion.** Havlík grounds meaning in a corpus with nothing outside it to correct drift. Mandelkern & Linzen's Izzy refers perfectly and can check nothing. Ruyant has norms and no reliability. Pepp and Attah are about basic reference and intention, neither of which involves checking.

One honest exception, to be stated rather than papered over: Grindrod's Evansian **producers** "have other causal-informational channels open to the object" and thereby maintain the naming practice, while consumers do not. That is a correction route in embryo. He does not develop it as an epistemic notion, and developing it is our contribution -- but the paper should credit it as the nearest precedent instead of claiming a clean sweep. Comparison counts in Havlík for reference: `reliab*` 2, `correct*` 1, `calibrat*` 0, `error` 1.

**Bearing on the draft:** none of this changes the recommendation. The performativity reply works whether or not he is right, engaging a scoped live account is worth more than dismissing a wrong one, and a *Synthese* referee may have read him. If anything, (1) and (3) give §6 a second move after the performativity point: his own scope hedge and his own usage disjunction both bend toward the profile view.

### Second use: he sharpens the RLHF question into a three-way disagreement

Ruyant makes fine-tuning **constitutive** of the representational target: the norms are implemented in data cleanup and then, much more specifically, in instruction-tuning and human feedback, and "the norms thus implemented are no longer merely cognitive and linguistic."

Set against Grindrod, who argues fine-tuning is "a relatively marginal procedure" that cannot be the difference between intentionality and its lack, there is now a live disagreement in *Synthese* about what RLHF does. Our draft's prediction, that preference feedback improves conversational fit before answerability, is not a fourth opinion in that argument. It is a way of dissolving it: RLHF is constitutive of something real (appropriateness-for-purpose, which is Ruyant's target) and marginal to something else (world-directed correction, which is Grindrod's), because they are different stabilizers. Naming both and showing the profile view adjudicates is a strong, venue-local move, and it delivers precisely the "mechanism-conflict adjudication" that `DECISIONS.md` (2026-05-22) identified as load-bearing against the relabeling charge.

## Attah 2025: useful, secondary, and a boundary to respect

He targets the **CI Argument**: (1) genuine linguistic competence requires communicative intention; (2) LMs have no mechanism for communicative intentions; therefore LMs lack linguistic competence. Proponents cited: Bender & Koller, Bender et al., Shanahan, Montemayor.

He rejects both premises. On a strong Gricean conception the argument fails at premise 1, because that conception "is now known to be empirically untenable." On an attenuated conception (an intention to use language to trigger an illocutionary or perlocutionary effect) it fails at premise 2, because LMs can meet it.

His answer to the title question is "no," but with a caution worth quoting for our purposes: even granted, in most cases such intentions "are too lean to warrant our philosophical attention," and where they look substantial "the jury is still out."

### How we use him

As the fourth member of the concession cluster, not as a separate argument. Grindrod, Mandelkern & Linzen, Pepp and Attah between them close off the easy routes to LLM skepticism: not meaning, not reference, not intention. That is the strongest possible setup for our move, and citing all four in one sentence makes the point that the skeptic's remaining ground is answerability, which is ours.

**Do not overreach with him.** Attah is arguing about linguistic competence and intention. He says nothing about reliability or truth, and his own hedge (intentions "too lean to warrant philosophical attention") should be quoted rather than smoothed over, since a referee who knows the paper will notice if we recruit him as a stronger ally than he is.
