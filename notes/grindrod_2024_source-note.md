Source: Jumbly Grindrod, "Large language models and linguistic intentionality," *Synthese* 204:71 (2024). DOI: 10.1007/s11229-024-04723-8. Open access (CC-BY). Received 15 February 2024, accepted 22 July 2024.

## Why this one matters most

It is the closest live interlocutor at the target venue, and it is open access, so a referee or editor can check our treatment of it in one click.

## Grindrod's argument in brief

He asks the **meaningful usage question**: are LLM outputs instances of meaningful language use, setting aside intelligence, sentience, and consciousness (lines 108--116)?

His move is to switch metasemantic register. The dominant approach applies **mental** metasemantics (how a cognitive state gets its content); he argues we should apply **linguistic** metasemantics (how words, sentences, and utterances get theirs), because "LLMs obviously only process linguistic information and we then interact with LLM-powered chatbots linguistically" (lines 700--708).

Two results follow:

1. **Mental metasemantics returns a negative verdict** (§3). He sides with Butlin (2021) against Coelho Mollo and Millière: a pre-trained LLM has no appropriately world-involving function. He grants their causal-informational condition (our own link to Aristotle is text-mediated too, lines 583--591) but denies that fine-tuning supplies the teleosemantic condition.
2. **Linguistic metasemantics returns a positive verdict** (§4), on two independent theories: Evans's naming practices and Millikan's teleosemantics. What unites them is "that linguistic intentionality relies on a pre-existing meaningful system" (lines 797--800).

## Three things we can use directly

### 1. The producer/consumer distinction is our "inherited participation," already published in Synthese

Evans divides a naming practice into **producers**, who "have other causal-informational channels open to the object... they can directly interact with the object and have demonstrative and recognitional capabilities" and who update the practice, and **consumers**, who use the name by storing the practice's information (lines 834--846).

Grindrod's verdict on LLMs: "LLMs are quite unlike human speakers insofar as they are only ever consumers of linguistic practices. This nicely captures the idea that there is an important linguistic activity, where the relation between word and world is maintained, that LLMs do not take part in" (lines 906--911). And: "Those more direct interactions are an important part of linguistic practice that LLMs, as consumers, do not have access to" (lines 923--926).

That is our inherited-vs-operational distinction in Evansian clothing, arrived at independently and published at our target venue two years earlier. **We must cite it, and we should say what our version adds.** The honest answer: Evans's producer/consumer split is binary and name-indexed; our profile is graded, multi-route, and predicts *which* intervention repairs *which* deficit. Grindrod has one channel (direct interaction with the referent); we have seven stabilizing routes and a transfer prediction for each.

### Does the producer/consumer machinery do real epistemic work? (checked 2026-08-08)

Asked because the paper's correction claim needs to know whether Evans is a precedent to credit or a rival to answer. Verdict: **a genuine world-to-practice channel, but scoped to reference, not truth.**

*What it does.* More than I first allowed. Producers hold "other causal-informational channels open to the object," and "the community's naming practice then gets **updated** by the interactions that producers have with the named object" (839). Grindrod states the exclusion outright: "there is an important linguistic activity, where **the relation between word and world is maintained**, that LLMs do not take part in" (908--909). That is a structural maintenance claim, at our target venue, and it is the nearest published precedent for our thesis. Credit it as such.

*What it does not do.* Evans's practice tolerates falsehood by construction: "it may still be the case (as we are fallible creatures) that some of the information that forms part of the practice **is not true of the object**" (831--832). The practice fixes which object the name picks out; it does not make the associated information true. Producers update, they do not audit. A naming practice can be perfectly maintained, correctly anchored to Aristotle, and full of falsehoods about him.

**Evans therefore supplies our distinction himself: reference-anchoring and truth-answerability are different jobs, and his machinery does only the first.** That credits the precedent and marks the gap in one move, with no strawmanning, since the scope is his own.

*Vocabulary check, whole paper (24 pp):* `error` 0, `reliab*` 0, `check` 0, `calibrat*` 0, `drift` 0, `accura*` 0, `revis*` 0; `maintain` 1, `update` 1. Maintenance is asserted twice, analysed never. "Not developed epistemically" is now evidenced rather than impressionistic.

### ⚠ The best thing in this paper for us: a loop Grindrod opens and drops (914--921)

Evans conceded the producer/consumer split is "inexact in some respects" (Evans 1982, p. 377): consumers *can* change a naming practice given enough influence. Grindrod's illustration is "an influential ancient historian succeeds in circulating a falsehood about Aristotle," followed by:

> "it would not be impossible either for **LLMs to effect a change in a naming practice** provided that they could exert the requisite influence."

Two sentences, then he returns to the main line. That is **influence without anchoring**: a consumer that never participates in maintenance, writing into the practice at scale, with no route by which the resulting error is caught.

This converges with Ruyant's performativity clause from the opposite direction (Ruyant: fictions become actual texts; Grindrod: influential consumers reshape practices). Both describe LLM output entering the human corpus; neither has a correction account. **Taking the loop Grindrod leaves open is a stronger §6 move than answering him**, and it lets §6 handle Grindrod and Ruyant with one mechanism.

### Source caution, and the Madagascar question resolved (2026-08-08)

**Gareth Evans, *Varieties of Reference* (1982), is not in `literature/`** (the `evans2009myth` files are Vyvyan Evans, a different person), and is borrow-only on Internet Archive (`varietiesofrefer0000evan`, encrypted PDFs, search-inside auth-gated). Everything above is Grindrod's presentation of Evans, with p. 377 his cite for the "inexact" concession.

**That turns out not to matter, and an error was caught on the way.** Madagascar is **Evans 1973, "The Causal Theory of Names," *Proceedings of the Aristotelian Society* 47** ~-- a journal article ~-- not the 1982 book. Grindrod's "Evans (1973, 1982)" bundles them, and I was about to send us after the wrong item.

The inward check settled it: **Pepp 2025 states the case precisely**, and she is on our exact topic, already in the corpus and the central bib, which makes her the better citation than Evans for an LLM paper:

> "Famously, Gareth Evans's (1973) 'Madagascar' critique of Kripke's picture cast doubt on reference-preserving intentions' being sufficient to keep the reference the same when a name is passed from speaker to speaker... Evans, inspired by Isaac Taylor (1896), considers a case in which the name 'Madagascar' was transmitted by a Malay sailor, whose use of the name referred to a part of the African mainland, to Marco Polo, who took it to refer, instead, to the island we use it to refer to today. Although Marco Polo intended to use the name in the same way as the sailors, and others intended to use it in the same way as Marco Polo, and so on, **reference was not preserved**."

That is the point we wanted, in a citable form: a naming practice in which reference drifted while every participant intended to preserve it, and nothing in the practice caught the drift. It restabilised on a new referent. Maintenance without error-detection.

⚠ **Carry Pepp's caveat.** She notes that "as commentators on Evans' case have noted, the highly compressed story presented by Evans is not historically accurate. But it serves to make the point anyway." Use Madagascar as a philosophical illustration, never as a historical claim; a source-grounding-minded referee will check.

**Also acquired:** deRosset, L. (2020), "Reference Switch and Bad Dubbings," 34 pp, now at `literature/derosset_2020_reference_switch_and_bad_dubbings.{pdf,md}` (open PDF from uvm.edu, cited in Pepp's bibliography). A full treatment of reference switch and a solution via meta-semantic deference. **Probably not needed:** it is deep reference theory, and our topic is answerability, so citing it risks scope creep. On disk if the §6 argument turns out to need the switch phenomenon analysed rather than illustrated.

**Net: nothing is blocked.** Grindrod carries the producer/consumer point, Pepp carries Madagascar, and Evans 1982 is optional ~-- wanted only if we quote the producer/consumer machinery directly, which for an LLM paper is the weaker citation anyway.

### 2. He is an ally, not a rival, on the central architecture

Grindrod's explanandum is **meaningfulness**; ours is **truth-tracking answerability**. He can grant everything we say. Better: his §3 result (no world-involving function from pre-training) and his §4 result (meaning nonetheless, via the inherited system) *jointly entail the shape of our thesis* -- meaning can be inherited whole while world-directed correction is not inherited at all.

This lets §7 make the concessive move much more cheaply than the current draft does. Rather than arguing against Coelho Mollo and Millière, we can say: grant Grindrod's positive verdict on meaning; the question we ask is what stabilizes the *truth* of what is meant, and that is where the profile splits.

### 3. His RLHF argument converges with ours, from a different direction

The draft's §7 predicts that preference feedback "should improve conversational fit before it improves answerability." Grindrod argues that fine-tuning cannot be what confers intentionality, because "it would be odd to claim that a relatively marginal procedure that is designed to optimize the performance of a LLM on specific tasks ends up being the difference between intentionality and its lack" (lines 659--661), citing Lederman and Mahowald: "while RLHF influences model capabilities, even models without RLHF produce apparently meaningful text" (lines 683--685).

Same conclusion, different lever. He argues RLHF is too marginal to confer intentionality; we argue it strengthens the wrong stabilizer. Citing him here converts one of our bare predictions into a prediction with independent support.

## ⚠ The terminology collision, and it is serious

**Millikan's technical term for the function a linguistic device serves is a "stabilizing function."** Grindrod uses it throughout §4.2:

> "Millikan describes the type of function that linguistic devices serve as 'stabilizing functions.' Stabilizing functions reside between two systems -- in this case, between speaker and hearer -- and have the effect of encouraging each system to keep using that device in order to elicit the same response." (lines 969--975)

> "what separates meaningful from meaningless tokens of a word is that the stabilizing function serves as part of the explanation as to why it has been reproduced on that occasion." (lines 1020--1022)

Also at lines 1029 and 1045.

Our paper's central term is **stabilizer** / **stabilizing route** / **stabilizing mechanism**, defined as a route that "lets facts, or practices already adjusted to facts, constrain what a system produces and how its errors are corrected" (abstract).

These are not the same notion. Millikan's stabilizing function is **conventional and inter-agent**: it sits between speaker and hearer and explains why a linguistic device keeps getting reproduced. Ours is **epistemic and world-directed**: it explains why a representation is answerable to how things are. A device could have a robust Millikanian stabilizing function while participating in none of our stabilizers, which is arguably exactly the LLM case, and is a genuinely interesting point rather than an embarrassment.

But as the draft stands, a Millikan-literate Synthese referee meets the word *stabilizer* on page one of a paper about LLM meaning and reads it as either a confused borrowing or an unacknowledged reinvention. This is the **relabeling charge** that `DECISIONS.md` has flagged as the paper's top risk since 2026-05-22, now with a specific citable source at the target venue to hang it on.

**Recommended fix (Brett's call):** a footnote at first use of *stabilizer* distinguishing it from Millikan's *stabilizing function*, plus one sentence in §7 turning the distinction into an argument: a Millikanian stabilizing function is exactly what LLMs inherit in full, and exactly what fails to secure answerability. That converts the collision from a liability into a payoff. Renaming our term is the alternative, but it would cost the whole draft and I don't think it is warranted.

## Leads picked up from Grindrod's references

- **Lederman & Mahowald (2024)**, "Are language models more like libraries or library patrons?" -- bibliotechnism; the basic/derived meaning distinction (his fn. 4, lines 135--139). Not in `literature/`. Relevant to our inherited-participation category.
- **Butlin (2021)** on teleosemantics and LLM content; note Grindrod's fn. 23 says Butlin has since changed his position, so cite the 2021 time-slice carefully if at all.
- **Michaelson (2023)** futurism; **Michaelson & Nowak (2022)** semantic-value pluralism (lines 1081--1099). The pluralism point is structurally close to our profile move (one token, several values, different routes) and might be worth a sentence.
- **Cappelen & Dever (2021)**, *Making AI Intelligible* -- externalism means internal inspection may not settle the question (lines 152--158).
